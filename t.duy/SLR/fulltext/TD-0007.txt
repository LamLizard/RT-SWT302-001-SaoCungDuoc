ABSTRACT

The automation of software requirements analysis and test case generation remains one of the

most pressing challenges in modern software engineering. Traditional approaches rely heavily on
manual effort, which is time-consuming, error-prone, and inconsistent, especially as systems grow

in complexity. This paper presents SmartSE, a novel AI-driven framework that leverages Large

Language Models (LLMs), specifically fine-tuned variants of GPT-4 and LLaMA-3, to automate

two critical phases of the software development lifecycle (SDLC): (1) natural language requirements

analysis and formalization and (2) intelligent test case generation. The proposed framework integrates
a multi-stage pipeline comprising requirement parsing, ambiguity detection, semantic enrichment,

and test oracle synthesis. The authors evaluate SmartSE on three real-world open-source projects,

Apache Kafka, Mozilla Firefox, and OpenMRS, spanning over 14,000 requirement statements and

21,000 existing test cases.

INTRODUCTION

The software industry faces the consequences of rapid change in software systems, with current

conditions placing greater stress on software engineering (SE) than ever. Increased complexity,

distributed architecture, and dynamically shifting user needs have made building and testing reliable

software more difficult than ever before. These phenomena, among others, have made it increasingly

difficult to construct reliable, well-tested software. Given the tools developed and the decades of
research conducted, it is surprising that two phases of the software development lifecycle remain

especially resource-intensive: requirements engineering and software testing. Pressman and Maxim

(2020) and Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) Computer Society (2023) cited

industry standards stating that requirements-related defects account for 40–60% of software errors,

while testing consumes 30–50% of the project budget.

The process of requirements engineering (i.e., eliciting, analyzing, documenting, and validating

system requirements) is language-oriented. Stakeholders tend to describe their needs in imprecise

natural language to capture their thoughts. Leading to the absence of precision, and the description
may lack the completeness to fully describe the need. Historically, attempts to automate requirements

analysis have relied on dominant natural language processing (NLP) frameworks, keyword-based

systems, or basic machine learning approaches. Such tools have been shown to have issues with

generalization, handling domain-specific phrases, and reasoning about requirements that must be

inferred (Arora et al., 2019).
Conventional methods for generating test cases, whether based on a specification or on code, share

similar issues. They are unable to keep pace with the growing complexity of software and often do

not achieve the necessary semantic coverage. Large language models (LLMs) have ushered in a new

era of understanding and generating human-like language. They include GPT-4o (Hurst et al., 2024),

LLaMA-3 (Meta AI, 2024), Gemini (Google DeepMind, 2024), and DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI,
2025), which display extraordinary abilities in understanding and summarizing text, reasoning, and

generating code. DeepSeek-R1 adopts a new reinforcement-learning-based reasoning paradigm,

achieving results like OpenAI’s o1 on coding and science, technology, engineering, and mathematics

(STEM) benchmarks, and is fully open-weight, creating new avenues for inexpensive and focused

fine-tuning. Their in-context learning, ability to be fine-tuned on domain-specific corpora, and
capacity for retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) make them well-suited to addressing persistent

issues in SE (Ferrari et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024).

We present SmartSE, a broad artificial intelligence (AI) system built on LLMs to fully automate

both requirements analysis and test case generation. The system functions as an end-to-end pipeline,

starting with the ingestion of raw, unstructured requirement documents, followed by multi-stage
NLP processing to detect ambiguity, extract semantic constraints, and generate cleaned requirement

documents. It then uses the cleaned documents to generate high-quality executable test cases for both

functional and edge cases.

This paper presents the following main contributions.

• a novel integrative LLM-based framework, SmartSE, that extends the boundary of integration of

requirements engineering and test case generation and offers a unified architecture to both fields
• an innovative domain-adapted fine-tuning approach for GPT-4 and LLaMA-3 in the SE domain,

yielding significant advancements in requirements disambiguation and test oracle generation

• an extensive evaluation of SmartSE on three large, real-world open-source software projects,

providing a significant improvement to the current state of the art

• a large-scale and extensive usability evaluation of SmartSE in real-life SE projects
The rest of the paper includes discussions of related work, the SmartSE framework, the

experimental setup, the experiment results, and the findings, including their implications, constraints,

and directions for future research.

RELATED WORK

Related research covers three areas: requirements engineering methods using NLP/transformer

models (including studies based on BERT, RoBERTa, and recent LLMs on disambiguation);
automated test case generation, from symbolic execution and search-based methods to GPT-driven

unit test generation and mutation-driven test creation; and the broader use of LLMs in SE tasks and

pinpointing the gaps that SmartSE addresses. Integrating NLP into requirements engineering is not

an original concept. The first methodologies used keyword-centric methods and parsing algorithms

to identify requirements, actors, and constraints (Koff, 2004; Slankas & Williams, 2013). This was
followed by machine learning methods, including support vector machines (Cleland-Huang et al.,

2012) and random forests, for requirements classification (Casamayor et al., 2010). The most notable

recent approaches use transformers, such as BERT and RoBERTa. These transformers have been

successfully applied to requirements with traceability, ambiguity, and completeness (M. Cheng et
al., 2021; Fischbach et al., 2021).

The last few years have seen rapid growth in LLM-focused requirements engineering (RE)

methodologies. Fantechi et al. (2024) noted that while GPT-4 can identify ambiguities, inconsistencies,

and incompleteness in requirements, the existence of requirement smells remains a matter of debate.

In a systematic literature review of 74 primary studies, Ferrari et al. (2025) found that LLM-based RE
literature has seen unprecedented growth since 2023, with most activity centered on elicitation and

validation and markedly less on defect detection. According to Bashir et al. (2025), few-shot in-context

learning with LLMs outperforms zero-shot learning by 20.2% on industrial ambiguity-detection

datasets and generates actionable explanations that domain experts appreciate. Tehrani et al. (2025)

noted further cataloged research directions for LLMs within the full RE lifecycle, particularly
promising in disambiguation, completeness checking, and requirements-to-model traceability.

Most prior studies, however, treat requirements engineering tasks in isolation, whether

classification, defect detection, or formalization. They do not offer an integrated pipeline that

encompasses the entire requirements analysis process. Ferrari and Gnesi (2025) present a two-sided

roadmap for integrating formal requirements with LLMs, asserting that formal verification can
certify the correctness of LLM-enhanced RE activities. SmartSE addresses this integration gap by

consolidating these sub-tasks into a single LLM-based approach, facilitating contextual cross-task

reasoning, and aligning with the growing belief that combining hybrid LLM and structured systems

is the most promising approach.

Automated test case generation across multiple paradigms has attracted considerable research
effort, including symbolic execution (Cadar et al., 2008; King, 1976), search-based software

testing (Fraser & Arcuri, 2011; Harman & Jones, 2001), and model-based testing (Pretschner et

al., 2005). EvoSuite (Fraser & Arcuri, 2011) and RANDOOP (Pacheco et al., 2007) are tools that

generate test cases from bytecode, but their coverage is limited to structural aspects, and they cannot

capture high-level semantic intent. Recently, deep learning has been applied to test generation. New
techniques using neural machine translation models to translate specifications into test scripts have

been documented (Tufano et al., 2021), as has the use of Codex/GPT to generate unit tests (Yuan et

al., 2023; Schafer et al., 2023).

One of the most extensive surveys was conducted by Wang et al. (2024), who analyzed 102 papers

on the application of LLMs to software testing, with the most relevant application domains being
test case generation and code repair. Alagarsamy et al. (2025) generated test cases from text using a

fine-tuned GPT-3.5, among eight other LLMs, on a corpus of 163,000 pairs from five open-source

projects, achieving 78.5% syntactic correctness and 61.7% code coverage. Dakhel et al. (2024)

developed MuTAP; the authors show that adding surviving mutation operators to LLM prompts is a

significant method for improving test fault detection, achieving a mutation score of 93.57% on synthetic
faults. This exemplifies the significant impact that a post-processing step can have on closing the gap

between tests generated by LLMs and those written by humans. An industry case study by Masuda

et al. (2025) analyzed the generation of high-level test cases from natural-language requirements

using GPT-4o and found a high degree of correspondence between the tested and true test cases in

the embedded systems and Bluetooth domains. These works are a step in the right direction, but they
focus on code-level generation without working with upstream requirements artifacts. Our framework

furthers this line of work by linking test generation to formal specification and explicitly integrating

ambiguity detection into test synthesis.

The LLM application for SE tasks has seen significant acceleration following the launch of

GitHub Copilot (M. Chen et al., 2021) and subsequent work on code generation, summarization, and
bug fixing. Interpretable machine learning (ML) frameworks have also found growing application in

content moderation and social media integrity tasks, such as spambot and fake follower detection on

platforms such as X (formerly Twitter), with Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) and Local

competitive performance on coding tasks can be achieved at drastically lower costs. Furthermore,

requirement-clarification loop, and showed that resolving requirement ambiguities significantly

reduced generation errors. Nevertheless, none of the systems approaches the entire process from

unstructured requirements to executable tests. None includes specific stages for ambiguity detection

and requirements formalization, both of which are integral to SmartSE.

THE SMARTSE FRAMEWORK

The following describes the five-stage pipeline: requirements ingestion and preprocessing;
ambiguity detection and resolution (using fine-tuned LLaMA-3-8B); semantic formalization—with

GPT-4o employing chain-of-thought (CoT) prompting; test case synthesis (using RAG-augmented

GPT-4 to generate JUnit/pytest tests); and validation with coverage analysis. It demonstrates how

each module transforms raw requirement documents into validated, traceable test suites.

Framework Overview

SmartSE is designed as a modular, five-stage pipeline that transforms unstructured natural
language requirement documents into structured specifications and high-quality test cases. Figure

1 illustrates the overall architecture. The pipeline consists of the following modules: requirements

ingestion and preprocessing, ambiguity detection and resolution, semantic formalization, test case

synthesis, and validation and coverage analysis.

Module 1: Requirements Ingestion and Preprocessing

The ingestion module supports various file formats, including PDFs, .docx files, plain text, and
Jira exports. A format-independent parsing layer transforms raw text into segmented requirements

units (SRUs), which are single sentences or clauses that describe testable behavior. Based on the

PURE dataset (Ferrari et al., 2017) and 3,200 augmented documents, including software requirements

documents, a model for detecting sentence boundaries has been developed. Each SRU is identified

with a preliminary tag corresponding to one of the following: functional, non-functional, constraint,
assumption, or glossary. This is done using a BERT-based classifier fine-tuned to the corresponding

labeled requirement corpora. These preliminary tags help classify and route SRUs to downstream

processing types, which route them to specific sub-modules for analysis.

Module 2: Detecting and Resolving Ambiguities in Requirements

Uncertainty in requirements is a leading cause of software errors. There are four types of
ambiguity common to natural language requirements identified by the SmartSE module for ambiguity

detection: lexical ambiguity (multiple meanings for a word), syntactic ambiguity (phrases with more

than one potential parse tree), semantic ambiguity (statements that can be interpreted multiple ways),

and referential ambiguity (unclear references to pronouns or entities). LLaMA-3-8B is trained on

a dataset of 9,400 manually labeled requirement sentences across all four types of ambiguity to
detect ambiguity. The 9,400-sentence corpus was annotated by three independent SE researchers

using a codebook developed from the International Organization for Standardization/International

Electrotechnical Commission (ISO/IEC) 29148 standard, yielding an inter-annotator agreement

(Cohen’s κ) of 0.82 across all four ambiguity types. Fine-tuning was performed for three epochs with

a learning rate of 2e-5, batch size of 16, and a linear warm-up over the first 10% of steps, on four
Nvidia A100 80-gigabyte graphics processing units. A few-shot in-context learning model performed

better than zero-shot prompting, while a GPT-4 class model reliably identified lexical and semantic

Module 3: Semantic Formalization

Before robust test cases can be created, requirements must be formalized. Using a domain-specific
ontology, disambiguated analyzed requirement units (ARUs) are converted into structured requirement

templates. There are four components to each template: a subject (actor or system component), an

action (required behavior), an object (target artifact or data), and a precondition (trigger condition). An

example of GPT-4o (Hurst et al., 2024) formalizes this approach using a CoT instructional paradigm.

Compared to GPT-4-turbo, GPT-4o shows improved delta multimodal contextualization and fewer
hallucinations on structured-output marginal tasks. Several exemplars from the SmartSE training

corpus are presented, along with the requirement text and the resolved type label. Using the ClarifyGPT

framework, Mu et al. (2024) found that defining the clarification intent in the prompt markedly

reduced misunderstandings arising from incorrect specifications. For ontological consistency, output

templates are checked against a JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) schema

Module 4: Test Case Synthesis
The test synthesis module can produce executable test cases in various formats, such as

natural language test procedures (for manual execution), JUnit 5 test methods (for Java systems),

and pytest fixtures (for Python systems), based on a proven template of formalized requirements.

Synthesis is performed using a fine-tuned GPT-4 model based on the defined template, the relevant

programming language and testing framework, project-specific context from the codebase via RAG,
and a comprehensive set of testing heuristics encompassing boundary value analysis, equivalence

partitioning, and error guessing. The RAG component uses text-embedding-ada-002 embeddings with

512-token chunks (64-token overlap), a top-5 cosine-similarity retrieval strategy, and a Facebook

AI Similarity Search (FAISS) vector index built from the target codebase. Prompt templates follow

a structured CoT format: the system prompt establishes the role of a senior quality assurance (QA)
engineer; the user turn provides the formalized requirement template, retrieved code context, and the

testing heuristic class to apply; and the model is instructed to produce a self-contained, executable

test case with an inline natural-language rationale. Each test case generated is associated with the

originating requirement ID, along with the test type (positive, negative, boundary, or performance),
along with the expected contribution to coverage (statement, branch, or path), and a natural language

explanation of the test’s intentions. The extensive metadata supports the direct tracing of requirements

to tests, which is critical in regulated industries.

Module 5: Validation and Coverage Analysis

The last module validates twice. The first pass uses static analysis tools (Checkstyle for Java,

pylint for Python) to validate the syntactic correctness of the generated test code. The second pass
uses a semantic consistency checker to verify that the expected results for each test case correspond

to the post-condition specified for that test in the requirement template. Tests that fail either of the

two checks are flagged for human review. Coverage analysis is performed by running the generated

tests against the target codebase with either JaCoCo (for Java) or Coverage.py (for Python), and the

results are presented in a requirements-to-coverage traceability matrix. This matrix allows project
managers to see which requirements are not adequately covered, enabling risk-based testing.

Baseline Methods

SmartSE is compared against five baseline systems representing the current state of practice:

• GPT-3.5-Turbo (zero-shot): standard prompt-based requirements analysis and test generation

without fine-tuning or RAG
• BERT-Classify: a BERT-base-uncased model fine-tuned for requirements classification and

ambiguity detection

• EvoSuite: a search-based test generation tool operating on compiled bytecode

• ChatUniTest: an LLM-based unit test generator using a repair-based feedback loop

• rule-based NLP: a traditional pipeline using Stanford CoreNLP with hand-crafted rules for

requirements formalization

RESULTS AND ANALYSIS

Reports indicate that SmartSE outperforms all baselines across every metric-achieving 91.3%

RCS (14.3 points above GPT-3.5), 88.7% branch coverage, a mutation score of 74.3%, and a defect
prediction F1 of 0.89, all with statistically significant differences. Additionally, a user study confirms

43% less rework and 57% faster test authoring.

User Study Results

In total, 32 professional software engineers (mean experience: 6.4 years, SD = 2.1) participated

in a controlled user study comparing SmartSE-assisted development against their standard workflow.

Participants were recruited voluntarily from two software development firms in Saudi Arabia and one

academic research group, spanning roles including requirements analysts, software architects, and
quality assurance (QA) engineers. Each participant completed two structured tasks: disambiguating a

set of 20 natural-language requirements and authoring test cases for a provided specification module,

each within a two-hour session. A within-subjects design was used, with half the participants starting

with the SmartSE-assisted condition and half with the manual condition (counterbalanced to reduce

order effects). Participants reported a 43% reduction in requirements-related rework cycles and a 57%
reduction in time spent authoring test cases. On a five-point Likert scale, participants rated SmartSE’s

output quality at 4.3/5.0 and ease of integration with existing workflows at 4.1/5.0. The regional and

organizational scope of the sample is acknowledged as a limitation; a larger cross-organizational

replication study is planned as future work.

DISCUSSION

The following considers the results and their practical implications, including the value of
fine-tuning and RAG, traceability benefits for regulated industries, and the communication advantages

of disambiguation reports. It also recognizes limitations, including English-only assessment,

open-source bias, remaining mutation gaps, and hallucination risks. Furthermore, it highlights future

directions, including multilingual support, integration of formal verification, reinforcement learning

(RL)-based self-improvement, multi-agent orchestration, and an open-source release. SmartSE’s results
carry important implications for SE practice. First, integrating LLMs with domain-specific fine-tuning

and RAG yields substantially better performance than zero-shot prompting—a finding that reinforces

the value of investing in curated, domain-specific training data. This is consistent with the broader

trend documented by Hou et al. (2024), who observed that fine-tuned or retrieval-augmented LLMs

consistently outperform zero-shot baselines on SE tasks by 10–25 percentage points.
Second, the requirements-to-test traceability produced by SmartSE addresses a longstanding

quality assurance challenge in regulated industries (e.g., healthcare, aerospace, and finance), where

demonstrating that every requirement is covered by at least one test is a regulatory obligation.

As AI-generated data proliferates, robust governance frameworks are increasingly critical for

managing the volume, quality, and compliance of data produced by intelligent systems (Bena et

al., 2025). This is particularly pertinent given the findings of Augusto et al. (2025), who confirmed

that LLM-generated system tests achieve defect-detection performance comparable to that of

human-authored tests in safety-critical domains when grounded in formal requirements. Similarly,

structured frameworks for evidence collection and analysis are essential when AI systems are deployed
in cloud-based environments that must support forensic accountability (Al-mugern et al., 2024).

Third, the user study findings suggest that SmartSE’s value is not confined to automation alone:

engineers report that the disambiguation reports serve as a valuable communication artifact between

business analysts and development teams, independently of the generated tests. Beyond SE, LLMs

are demonstrating predictive utility across high-stakes domains, including healthcare applications
such as recurrent fragility fracture prediction, where models such as LLaMA and Gamma achieve

accuracy exceeding 91% (Al-mugern et al., 2025), further underscoring the cross-domain potential

of LLM-based analytics. This observation aligns with Terragni et al. (2025), who argue that the

most impactful near-term LLM applications in SE are those that augment rather than replace human

judgment, particularly at the requirements-to-design boundary.
Several limitations of the current work merit acknowledgment. Following the Wohlin et

al. threats-to-validity framework, we organize these as follows. First, SmartSE’s performance

on requirements expressed in languages other than English has not been evaluated; LLMs vary

significantly in their multilingual capability, and the framework’s disambiguation module relies

on English-language linguistic patterns. This gap was noted by Ferrari et al. (2025), who found
that fewer than 8% of LLM-based RE studies use non-English datasets. Second, the framework has

been evaluated exclusively on open-source systems; proprietary enterprise codebases may exhibit

different requirement styles, naming conventions, and implicit domain knowledge that could affect

performance. Third, the mutation score of 74.3%, while state-of-the-art, indicates that SmartSE does

not yet achieve complete fault detection, particularly for complex, data-dependent defects and race
conditions in concurrent systems. This mirrors findings by Dakhel et al. (2024), who observed that

LLM-generated tests systematically underperform on mutation operators involving variable aliasing

and concurrency. Fourth, LLM hallucination remains a residual risk: Tian et al. (2025) showed that

even state-of-the-art models can misinterpret specifications in subtle ways that are difficult to catch

without formal verification, motivating the runtime consistency checks in SmartSE’s Module 5.
Finally, the study’s sample, though professionally experienced, was recruited from a limited regional

and organizational context, which may limit the generalizability of usability findings.

Several research directions emerge from this work. First, we plan to extend SmartSE with

multilingual requirement processing capability, leveraging multilingual LLMs such as mBERT, mT5,

and the multilingual variants of LLaMA-3 identified by Ferrari et al. (2025) as promising candidates
for cross-lingual RE tasks. Second, we intend to investigate the integration of formal verification

techniques (e.g., model checking and bounded model checking via Boolean satisfiability (SAT)/

satisfiability modulo theories (SMT) solvers) with LLM-generated specifications to provide stronger

correctness guarantees, following the hybrid LLM-plus-formal roadmap proposed by Ferrari and Gnesi

(2025). Third, inspired by the reinforcement learning approach of DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI, 2025),
we are exploring whether reinforcement learning (RL)-from-feedback can be applied to SmartSE’s

disambiguation and formalization modules, enabling the framework to improve its own specification

reasoning without labeled training data.

Fourth, we are developing an agentic orchestration layer in which specialized sub-agents

autonomously handle discrete pipeline stages, consistent with the multi-agent SE vision articulated
by Terragni et al. (2025). Fifth, we plan to incorporate mutation-guided test augmentation, inspired

by MuTAP (Dakhel et al., 2024), to target the residual gap in SmartSE’s mutation score by identifying

surviving operators and generating targeted counterexample tests. Finally, we plan to release the

CONCLUSION

This paper presents SmartSE, an end-to-end AI framework for automated software requirements

analysis and test case generation using LLMs. By integrating fine-tuned GPT-4o and LLaMA-3 models
into a multi-stage pipeline that encompasses ingestion, ambiguity detection, semantic formalization,

test synthesis, and coverage validation, SmartSE addresses a significant gap in productivity and quality

in modern software development. Evaluated on three large-scale real-world systems comprising over

14,000 requirement statements, SmartSE achieved an RCS of 91.3%, a branch coverage rate of 88.7%,

and a defect prediction F1-score of 0.89, all substantially exceeding state-of-the-art baselines. A
controlled user study further confirms the practical utility of the framework, with engineers reporting

significant reductions in both rework and test authoring effort.

As the LLM landscape continues to evolve rapidly (with powerful open-weight reasoning models

such as DeepSeek-R1, more capable multimodal architectures, and emerging agentic orchestration

paradigms the SmartSE architecture is designed to remain model-agnostic and extensible, allowing
its individual pipeline modules to be upgraded as superior backbones become available. SmartSE

represents a meaningful step toward the vision of AI-augmented SE, in which intelligent systems not

only accelerate development but also actively enhance software quality and team communication,

aligning with broader research directions identified by prior researchers.
